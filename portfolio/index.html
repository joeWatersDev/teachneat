Teachneat

Building, testing and retiring a classroom tool for teachers

Teachneat was a web app that gave secondary school teachers one class list for attendance, grouping, a name picker and personalized parent email, stored in a Google Sheet on the teacher's own Drive. I took it from discovery research to a live product with Google's OAuth verification, ran a structured test with colleagues, and sunset it in October 2026 when engagement data showed no evidence of a habit.

Discovery

Starting from measured friction, not a feature list

In January 2026 I surveyed teachers at a secondary school in the Waterloo Region District School Board (39 responses across math, English, science, physical education, guidance and special education), then ran four 15 to 20 minute follow-up interviews chosen to cover a range of teaching contexts. The survey quantified where non-instructional time went; the interviews explained why existing tools failed.

Three findings set the scope. Parent communication logistics, meaning finding past contact and assembling mass emails, was the most cited burden. The board's accommodation system was rated 2.4 out of 5, and its usability pushed teachers to consult it only at the start of a semester. Group formation tools worked well enough (3.75 out of 5), which marked grouping as a convenience rather than a pain point.

"I just need to know what the accommodations are. If I could just get a class list with the relevant accommodations, that would be great."Secondary teacher, interview

Definition

From findings to requirements with targets

The February product requirements document tied each module to a baseline from the research and a target the MVP had to hit, so the build could be judged against the problem instead of against its own feature list.

Pain pointResearch baselineMVP target

The same document listed its riskiest open question: whether a school board would permit accommodation data in a platform it had not approved. That question turned out to decide the product, and the section on what I would change starts there.

Product judgment

Decisions that shaped it

The teacher's Drive as the system of record

Student data lived in a spreadsheet the teacher owned, and the server kept no durable copy of it in the database, logs or backups. That one choice answered the first question a board's IT department asks, kept the requested Google scopes in the non-restricted tier, and avoided an annual third-party security assessment that a broader scope would have triggered.

A free tier designed around habit, a paid tier around analysis

Pricing was one plan at $6.99 a month or $69.90 a year (CAD) above a permanent free tier. The line was drawn so that the daily-use tools stayed free and the paid tier added statistics, higher email volume and grouping constraints, on the reasoning that a teacher who had formed a habit would pay for the analysis on top of it.

Removing the feature the research ranked most severe

A review of who may authorize a third-party tool to hold special education records concluded that the authority sits with the school board, and that a teacher's own consent transfers none of it. I removed accommodation tracking permanently, narrowed the launch to Ontario and made it free for the first year. It was the right legal call, and it also removed the module behind the research's strongest finding.

Instrumentation a school board could read

Usage was measured as monthly counts per tool plus the first date each account reached each milestone, with no event stream and no timestamps finer than a month. That was enough to answer the two questions that mattered (which tools teachers adopt, and whether use holds up) while staying simple to describe in a privacy review.

Validation

The test

With the product live and verified, I shared it with teaching colleagues at my school, explained what each tool did, and offered a gift card draw to anyone who submitted feedback. The test was built to answer one question before I committed to the costs of a public launch: would teachers who tried it come back on their own?

I measured it two ways. Behavior came from the product's own activation milestones and session records. Stated intent came from an anonymous exit poll asking how often each teacher would use each tool in a real school year, and how likely they were to create an account for one.

What testers did 2 tester accounts created
  • Both added a student within minutes of signing up
  • Both opened the Name Picker
  • Neither saved attendance, generated groups or sent an email
  • Each account recorded a single session
What testers said 2 poll responses as of Oct 18; poll closes Oct 23
  • Both rated their likelihood of creating an account at 4 out of 5
  • Attendance and grouping drew expected use of 3 to 5 out of 5
  • One respondent rated the Name Picker 1, "not useful"
  • One suggested grouping only the students present today

Stated intent and behavior pointed in opposite directions. Respondents expected to use attendance and grouping often, yet neither tester account saved an attendance register or generated a group, and the one tool both accounts did open was the one a respondent rated least useful. The poll was anonymous, so responses are not linked to accounts, but both came from the same small group.

The sample is far too small to estimate anything with confidence, and I have treated it that way. The test was not designed to measure a rate. It was designed to see whether the one signal a subscription business needs, unprompted repeat use, appeared at all among people who knew the developer and had an incentive to engage. It did not.

Decision

Why I stopped

Four pieces of evidence pointed the same way, and none of them depended on the small sample alone.

EvidenceWhat it meant for the business
The most severe researched pain point could not legally shipThe remaining tools solve problems teachers already rate as adequately served by free alternatives
Testers with context and an incentive did not returnA free-to-paid model depends on a daily habit forming first; none formed
No tester signed in with a school-managed Google accountReal use requires each board's IT department to allowlist the app, a sales cycle measured in months per board
Fixed costs arrive before revenueHosting alone ran about $31 USD a month, roughly seven subscribers at the planned price, before insurance, legal review and tax registration

The timing was deliberate. The test ran before any long-term commitment was made. Liability insurance, a lawyer's review of the legal documents, a business mailing address and tax registration were all scheduled and none had been signed. Total cash spent on the product was limited to hosting, the domain and the business-name registration.

Retrospective

What I would do differently

Test the riskiest assumption first

Board policy on accommodation data was open question two in the February requirements document. It was answered in August, after the module had been built, deployed and then removed. A week of conversations with a privacy officer in February would have reshaped the product before any code existed.

Measure retention before building infrastructure

Accounts, billing, encryption key management and two security reviews were necessary for a public launch and unnecessary for learning whether teachers would return. A spreadsheet prototype shared with ten teachers for a month would have produced the same retention signal at a fraction of the cost.

Treat distribution as part of the product

Teachers do most of their work in school-managed accounts that board IT controls. The path to those accounts, board allowlisting, was planned as a later phase. It should have been validated alongside the first prototype, because without it the product could only reach teachers' personal accounts.

Weight behavior over stated intent

I did, and it is the reason this ended in October rather than after a year of operating costs. The exit poll alone would have read as cautiously positive.
