Teachneat

Teachneat was a web app for secondary school teachers. It kept one class list in a Google Sheet on the teacher's own Drive and used it for attendance, random groups, a name picker and personalized emails to students and parents. I built it on my own between January and October 2026. It went live in August, passed Google's OAuth verification in September, and I shut it down in October after testing it with colleagues at my school.

Research

In January 2026 I surveyed teachers at my school in the Waterloo Region District School Board and got 39 responses from math, English, science, phys ed, guidance and special education. I then interviewed four of them for 15 to 20 minutes each, picking people who had given different kinds of answers.

Parent communication took the most time: about 74% of teachers spent more than five minutes a week finding past contact history and setting up emails before writing anything. Accommodations were the bigger risk: 70% said they had missed an accommodation they should have provided, and the board's system for looking them up averaged 2.4 out of 5. Most teachers were reasonably happy with how they made groups, usually with Flippity, and rated their current method 3.75 out of 5, so I treated grouping as a nice extra.

"I just need to know what the accommodations are. If I could just get a class list with the relevant accommodations, that would be great."Teacher interview, January 2026

Key decisions

Student data stays in the teacher's Google Drive

The class list lives in a Google Sheet that the teacher owns. The server reads and writes it through Google's API but doesn't keep a copy in its database, logs or backups. I chose this because it's the first thing a school board's IT department asks about. It also meant the app only needed Google's lower-risk permission scopes, which avoided the yearly third-party security audit that broader access requires.

Pricing

I planned a free account with the everyday tools and a Premium plan at $6.99 a month or $69.90 a year (CAD) that added attendance statistics, more email and extra grouping options. The idea was that teachers would get used to the free tools first and then pay for the extras. Billing was built and tested with Stripe but never switched on.

Removing accommodation tracking

In August I looked into who is allowed to approve a third-party tool for special education records. In every jurisdiction I checked, that authority belongs to the school board, and a teacher agreeing to the terms of service doesn't change that. I removed the accommodation feature completely and limited the launch to Ontario, free for the first year. I still think that was the right decision, but it meant losing the feature tied to the strongest result in my research.

Measuring usage

For each account I tracked the first date it reached each milestone (adding a student, saving attendance, making groups, opening the name picker, sending an email) and a monthly count of how often each tool was used. I kept it to monthly counts so it would be easy to explain in a school board privacy review. That was still enough to see which tools people used and whether they came back.

User testing

Once the app was live, I shared it with colleagues at my school, explained what each tool did, and entered everyone who filled out the feedback form into a gift card draw. Before paying for insurance, a legal review and the other things a public launch needed, I wanted to know whether teachers would keep using it after trying it.

I looked at two sources: what the accounts actually did, from the app's usage tracking, and what testers said in a feedback form about how often they would use each tool and how likely they were to sign up for real.

What testers did (3 accounts)

  • All three added students within minutes of signing up
  • All three opened the Name Picker, 6 times in total
  • None saved attendance, made groups or sent an email
  • One came back once, a week later

What testers said (3 responses)

  • Likelihood of signing up: 4, 4 and 2 out of 5
  • Grouping had the highest expected use: 5, 3 and 5
  • The Name Picker had the lowest: 1, 4 and 2
  • One teacher said they wouldn't take attendance twice, once for the school and again for the app
"This looks useful and I like how later in the email you can send it to the group of students with high absences, but if I was being honest I probably wouldn't take attendance twice (once for the school and then once for me)."Feedback form, October 2026

What testers said and what they did didn't line up. The form answers were mostly positive about attendance and grouping, but none of the accounts saved attendance or made a group, and the one tool everyone opened was the one rated lowest. The comment above explains the attendance result. Teachers already take attendance in the school's system, and doing it again in Teachneat isn't worth the time. The attendance statistics, the high-absence email list and the idea of grouping only students who are present all depend on that attendance being entered, so this is a bigger problem than one unused tool.

Form responses aren't linked to accounts, so I can't match what each person said to what their account did, but they all came from the same small group of people. Three people is far too few to measure anything with confidence. I was looking for something simpler, which was whether people who knew me, understood the tool and had a reason to try it would start using it regularly. Over the test period, none of them did.

Why I shut it down

I wasn't convinced the product had a path to making money that was worth the cost and risk, for the four reasons below.

What I foundWhat it meant
Accommodation tracking couldn't be offered legallyThe tools that were left solve problems teachers told me were already handled reasonably well, often by free tools like Flippity
Testers didn't use the core toolsThe free-to-paid plan depended on teachers using the free tools every day, and attendance would mean entering it twice
Nobody signed in with a school Google accountReaching teachers on their school accounts needs each board's IT department to approve the app, which takes months per board
Costs start well before revenueHosting alone was about $31 USD a month, or roughly seven paying subscribers, before insurance, a legal review and tax registration

I made the decision before committing to any of those costs. Insurance, the legal review, a business mailing address and tax registration were all planned but not started, so the total spent on the project was hosting, the domain and the business name registration.

What I would do differently

Answer the biggest risk first

Whether boards would allow accommodation data was an open question in my February requirements document. I didn't answer it until August, after the feature was built and live. A conversation with a board privacy officer in February would have changed the plan before I wrote any code.

Check where each feature's data comes from

Attendance looked like a strong feature on paper, but teachers already record it in the school's system. I should have asked, for each feature, whether teachers would have to enter the same information twice, and built around the answer.

Test whether people come back before building for launch

Accounts, billing, encryption key management and two security reviews were needed for a public launch, but not to find out whether teachers would use the tools. A simple prototype given to ten teachers for a month would have answered that much sooner and for much less.

Work out how to reach teachers early

Most teachers work in school-managed Google accounts that the board's IT department controls. I planned board approval as a later phase. I should have tested it alongside the first prototype, because without it the app can only reach teachers' personal accounts.
